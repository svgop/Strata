# Draft-head designs and how the field defines the objective: acceptance rate, acceptance length, tokens per step, or tokens per second

Primary-source research for the MAGMA v2 / Strata decision on lowering the MTP probability floor from 0.7 to
0.5 (throughput +8–11%, acceptance rate 91% → 87%, tokens banked per verify window 2.2 → 2.5, chain depth 4).

**(inference)**. Papers and repositories only; no marketing pages were used. Where a source could not be
retrieved, the gap is marked **NOT VERIFIED** rather than filled.

## 2. System sections

### Medusa

- **What it does differently**: Instead of acquiring a separate draft model, Medusa bolts K extra single-layer decoding heads onto the backbone's last hidden state and verifies a tree of candidate continuations in one forward pass; crucially, it is the source of the "typical acceptance" scheme Strata's rescue implements — "we propose employing a *typical acceptance* scheme to select plausible candidates rather than using rejection sampling" ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)).
- **Approach**: The criterion is `p_original(x_{n+k} | x_1…x_{n+k−1}) > min( ε, δ·exp(−H(p_original(· | x_1…x_{n+k−1}))) )`, "where H(·) denotes the entropy function, and ε, δ are the hard threshold and the entropy-dependent threshold respectively"; "a *prefix* of the candidate is accepted if it satisfies the condition", and "to guarantee the generation of at least one token at each step, we apply *greedy decoding* for the first token and *unconditionally* accept it" ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)). Heads are `p_t^(k) = softmax(W_2^(k)·(SiLU(W_1^(k)·h_t) + h_t))` with W_2 initialised from the LM head and W_1 zeroed ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)).
- **Key tradeoff**: Typical acceptance deliberately breaks the output distribution: "we ascertain that it is typically unnecessary to match the distribution of the original model", and the consequence is that it is *not* lossless — a fact EAGLE‑2 and EAGLE‑3 both use to exclude Medusa from their temperature=1 tables ("Methods like Medusa relax acceptance conditions under non-greedy settings, which do not guarantee lossless acceleration") ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3), [arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)). The repo states the same tradeoff as a feature: "Relaxing the requirement of matching the distribution of the original model makes the non-greedy generation even faster than greedy decoding" ([Medusa README](https://github.com/FasterDecoding/Medusa)).
- **What it got right that we have not**: Medusa's floor is not a floor — it is the *minimum of a fixed threshold and an entropy-scaled term*, so acceptance widens automatically exactly where the next-token distribution is flat and a fixed 0.7 would be arbitrary. It also guarantees progress with an unconditionally accepted first token per step, and it treats head count as a measured quantity ("five heads are sufficient at most… sometimes three or four heads may be enough") rather than a constant ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)).
- **Metric it publishes**: Wall-clock speedup, named "speedup". "Medusa‑1 can achieve over 2.2× speedup without compromising generation quality, while Medusa‑2 further improves the speedup to 2.3-3.6×" ([arXiv 2401.10774v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v1)); the v3 body instead says "Medusa can achieve a speedup of 2.3 to 2.8 times across different prompt types" ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)) — a version discrepancy worth knowing before quoting either number. Concrete measurements, "measuring in tokens processed per second": Medusa‑1 2.18× on Vicuna‑7B and 2.33× on Vicuna‑13B; Medusa‑2 2.83× on both, with coding at 3.29× and extraction at 3.62× ([arXiv 2401.10774](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.10774)). Setup: MT-Bench, batch size 1, "the use case where LLMs are locally hosted for personal use"; Medusa‑1 training "5 hours … on Vicuna 7B model with a single NVIDIA A100 PCIE GPU" ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3), [arXiv 2401.10774](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.10774)).
- **Sources**: [arXiv 2401.10774v3 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3) · [arXiv 2401.10774v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v1) · [arXiv 2401.10774 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774) · [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.10774) · [github.com/FasterDecoding/Medusa](https://github.com/FasterDecoding/Medusa)

### EAGLE (EAGLE, EAGLE‑2, EAGLE‑3)

- **What it does differently**: EAGLE drafts at the *feature* level rather than the token level and feeds in "a token sequence advanced by one time step" to resolve the sampling uncertainty that constrains feature autoregression; EAGLE‑2 drops the static tree for a "context-aware dynamic draft tree"; EAGLE‑3 "abandons feature prediction in favor of direct token prediction" and replaces top-layer features with multi-layer feature fusion via "training-time test" ([arXiv 2401.15077](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15077), [arXiv 2406.16858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16858), [arXiv 2503.01840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840)).
- **Approach**: EAGLE drafts `(f_1,f_2) + (t_2,t_3)` → `f_3` → `t_4`, concatenating each predicted feature with the sampled token to continue autoregressing; training combines `L_reg = Smooth L1(f_{i+1}, Draft_Model(T_{2:i+1}, F_{1:i}))` with a cross-entropy term at `w_cls = 0.1`, plus uniform noise `U(−0.1, 0.1)` on features to limit error accumulation ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077)). EAGLE‑2 sizes the tree by calibrated confidence: global value `V_i = Π_{t_j ∈ Path(root,t_i)} p_j ≈ Π c_j`, then top-k expansion and top-m reranking, "for nodes with the same value, we prioritize selecting shallower nodes" ([arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)).
- **Key tradeoff**: Tree drafting raises acceptance length far more than it raises speedup, and this is stated as a measured fact: "The implementation of tree draft and verification in EAGLE results in an approximate increase of 0.6-0.8 in the average acceptance length and about 0.3-0.5 in the speedup ratio… the improvement in the speedup ratio is less pronounced than the increase in average acceptance length" ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077)). At large batch, speculation must be throttled: EAGLE‑3 in SGLang "improves throughput by 40% at a batch size of 64", while the same authors note speculation "is often thought to reduce throughput at large batch sizes" ([arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)).
- **What it got right that we have not**: (a) It reports acceptance **per position** rather than as one blended ratio — EAGLE‑3's tables carry `0-α, 1-α, 2-α, 3-α, 4-α` alongside τ = 3.94 for Vicuna‑7B, and its diagnosis of EAGLE's data-scaling limit is precisely a *positional* observation: "the acceptance rate 0-α of the first draft token improves significantly. However… resulting in a very low acceptance rate 1-α for the second draft token" ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077), [arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)). (b) It makes the tree *dynamic* per context instead of fixed. (c) It reports acceptance length *and* speedup together, each with a stated limitation.
- **Metric it publishes**: Two metrics with verbatim definitions — "**Walltime speedup ratio: The actual test speedup ratio relative to vanilla autoregressive decoding**" and "**Average acceptance length τ: The average number of tokens accepted per forward pass of the target LLM**" (EAGLE); restated in EAGLE‑2 as "**Average Acceptance Length τ: The average number of tokens generated per drafting-verification cycle, which corresponds to the number of tokens accepted from the draft.**" EAGLE‑2 adds a cost note: "The advantage of average acceptance length is that it is independent of hardware and runtime environment, while its disadvantage is that it does not reflect the overhead of the draft model." EAGLE‑3's Table 1 caption is "Speedup ratios and average acceptance lengths τ of different methods" — and the table covers SpS, PLD, Medusa, Lookahead, Hydra, EAGLE, EAGLE‑2 and EAGLE‑3 with **no acceptance-rate column at all** ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077), [arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2), [arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)). Representative numbers with model and hardware: EAGLE‑3 on Vicuna‑13B, temperature 0, five-task mean **5.51× speedup with τ 6.62**, per task MT-bench 5.58×/τ 6.65, HumanEval 6.47×/τ 7.54, GSM8K 5.32×/τ 6.29, Alpaca 5.16×/τ 6.17, CNN/DM 5.01×/τ 6.47; EAGLE‑2 on the same model 4.22×/τ 4.83; Hydra 2.80×/τ 3.50; Medusa 2.12×/τ 2.51; Lookahead 1.62×/τ 1.67 ([arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)). Wall-clock on a single consumer card: "with gpt-fast, EAGLE accelerates LLaMA2-Chat 7B decoding to 160.4 tokens/s on a single RTX 3090 GPU", against 24.5 tokens/s vanilla HuggingFace and 55.1 tokens/s FP16 gpt-fast ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077)).
- **Sources**: [ar5iv EAGLE 2401.15077](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077) · [arXiv 2401.15077 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15077) · [arXiv 2406.16858v2 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2) · [arXiv 2406.16858 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16858) · [arXiv 2503.01840v3 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3) · [arXiv 2503.01840 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840)

### Hydra

- **What it does differently**: Hydra makes draft heads *sequentially dependent* — each head conditions on the tokens already speculated in the current candidate continuation, not only on the backbone's hidden state from verified tokens. All prior draft heads were "sequentially independent, meaning that they speculate tokens in the candidate continuation independently of any preceding tokens" ([Hydra abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05109)).
- **Approach**: Standard draft heads compute `p_draft(x_{t+i} | x_≤t+i−1) = f_{draft,i}(h_{t−1})`, which is a function only of the already generated sequence; Hydra computes `p_draft(x̂_{t+i} | x_≤t, x̂_{t+1}, …, x̂_{t+i−1}) = f_{Hydra,i}(h_{t−1}, x_t, x̂_{t+1}, …, x̂_{t+i−1})`. Hydra++ adds three changes: MLPs extended to 4 layers ("scaling to 5 layers and beyond provides no additional benefit"), a self-distillation objective predicting the base model's distribution, and an extra self-attention decoder layer queried once per decoding step ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)).
- **Key tradeoff**: The gain is real but modest on the ratio-of-tokens axis and only converts to a modest wall-clock gain: sequential dependence "increas[es] the average candidate continuation acceptance length by up to 0.46 tokens", which "corresponds to a significant improvement in decoding speeds, with Hydra head-based decoding achieving up to 1.1× better throughput than Medusa decoding". The advantage shrinks with batch: "for batch size 1 Hydra++ has a 2.70× improvement in throughput compared to standard decoding, but this gain decreases to 1.63× at batch size 8" ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)).
- **What it got right that we have not**: Hydra is the one system that (a) runs the *actual experiment* Strata is contemplating — sweeping the threshold itself — and (b) scores that sweep on acceptance length **and** judged quality rather than on an acceptance ratio: "We fix the sampling temperature τ=0.7, vary the posterior threshold ε ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25}, and set the posterior alpha as α = sqrt(ε)", and "We plot how varying the posterior threshold affects both the average speculation length and the quality of the resulting generations". It also separates *proposing* a tree from *selecting* it: trees are proposed to maximise expected acceptance length, then "we choose the optimal tree size for a given setup by empirically measuring the end-to-end throughput achieved using each T_i, and selecting the tree which maximizes throughput" ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)). **(inference)** Strata's adopted rule — six metrics may never regress, acceptance rate among them — is exactly the shortcut Hydra's protocol avoids, because Hydra never lets a ratio stand in for either the length or the wall-clock outcome.
- **Metric it publishes**: Two, defined in one sentence: "we report the average *throughput*, which is the number of tokens generated per second, and the average *acceptance length*, which is the number of tokens generated per decoding step, to evaluate the speed and quality of Hydra decoding" ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)). Numbers, Vicuna 7B/13B/33B on MT-Bench with greedy acceptance: Hydra 2.36×/2.17×/2.15× over autoregressive and 1.11×/1.10×/1.11× over Medusa; Hydra++ 2.70×/2.50×/2.53× over autoregressive and 1.27×/1.27×/1.31× over Medusa. Hardware: "all 7B and 13B parameter experiments on a single A100-40GB GPU and all 33B parameter experiments on a single A100-80GB GPU"; training on 8× A100-80GB. Tree budget: "we set the maximum tree size as N=100" over a 100-question Alpaca subset ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)).
- **Sources**: [arXiv 2402.05109v2 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2) · [arXiv 2402.05109 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05109) · [github.com/zankner/Hydra](https://github.com/zankner/hydra)

### Lookahead decoding

- **What it does differently**: It removes the draft model entirely. "We introduce Lookahead Decoding, an exact, parallel decoding algorithm that accelerates LLM decoding without needing auxiliary models or data stores"; it reformulates autoregressive decoding as a non-linear system solved by Jacobi iteration and harvests n-grams from the iteration trajectory into an n-gram pool ([arXiv 2402.02057v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02057v1)).
- **Approach**: A two-dimensional fixed window with `W` "the *lookahead* size into future token positions to conduct parallel decoding" and `N` "the *lookback* steps into the past Jacobi trajectory to retrieve n-grams"; a verification branch looks up n-grams "by checking if a n-gram starts with a token that exactly matches the last token of the current ongoing sequence" and verifies them in parallel, with "a cap of G to limit the maximum number of promising candidates… we simply set G=W". To keep the pool cheap, "we can force greedy sampling at the n-gram generation (lookahead branch), in which the probability distribution degenerates into a one-hot vector. Hence we only need to store which token is selected" ([arXiv 2402.02057v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02057v1)).
- **Key tradeoff**: Speedup is bought with FLOPs, and the exchange rate is poor for small models: "if W and N are too large, each lookahead decoding step might be too costly and slow down the decoding despite reducing decoding steps", and "The 7B, 13B, and 33B models require 120x, 80x, and 56x extra FLOPs per step, respectively. However, because of the memory-intensive bound characteristic of the LLM decoding, these extra FLOPs only bring little per-step cost". It is positioned for latency-critical work only: "lookahead decoding should be used in scenarios where latency is vital, e.g., surplus FLOPs exist that can be traded for latency" ([LMSYS blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-11-21-lookahead-decoding/)).
- **What it got right that we have not**: It publishes the **cost side of the depth decision** alongside the benefit — `W*(N−1) + G*(N−1)` input tokens per step and the 120×/80×/56× FLOP multipliers — so a deeper window is a priced decision rather than a free one. It also reports its own per-model operating point in a table ("7B → W=15, N=5; 13B → W=10, N=5; 33B → W=7, N=5 on A100") instead of one global setting, and it frames the whole design as a *scaling law between FLOPs and steps* rather than a single ratio ([LMSYS blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-11-21-lookahead-decoding/), [arXiv 2402.02057v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02057v1)). **(inference)** Its per-model W/N table is the closest published analogue to Strata needing a per-workload floor rather than one global floor.
- **Metric it publishes**: Wall-clock speedup and steps-per-token, not a ratio. Multi-GPU / code: "can speed up autoregressive decoding by up to 1.8x on MT-bench and 4x with strong scaling on multiple GPUs in code completion tasks" ([arXiv 2402.02057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02057)). Per task on the blog: ~1.5× (LLaMA-Chat, MT-Bench), >2× (CodeLLaMA, HumanEval), 1.8× (CodeLLaMA-Instruct, GSM8K), with hardware stated: "The 7B, 13B, and 33B models are evaluated on a single A100 GPU, and the 70B model is evaluated on two A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism, all under fp16 precision" ([LMSYS blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-11-21-lookahead-decoding/)). Independently measured by the EAGLE‑3 authors on Vicuna‑13B, temperature 0, five-task mean: **1.62× speedup with τ 1.67** — the lowest τ of the eight methods in that table ([arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)).
- **Sources**: [arXiv 2402.02057v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02057v1) · [arXiv 2402.02057 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02057) · [LMSYS blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-11-21-lookahead-decoding/) · [github.com/hao-ai-lab/LookaheadDecoding](https://github.com/hao-ai-lab/LookaheadDecoding)

### REST

- **What it does differently**: It replaces the parametric draft model with a non-parametric retrieval datastore. "Instead of relying on a parametric draft model, our method Retrieval-Based Speculative Decoding (REST) proposes using retrieval for draft construction"; "Its plug-and-play nature allows for seamless integration and acceleration of any language models, all without necessitating additional training" ([arXiv 2311.08252v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08252v2), [arXiv 2311.08252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08252)).
- **Approach**: A datastore `D = {(c_i, t_i)}` of context/continuation pairs is built from a corpus; at inference the longest suffix of the context is matched exactly using a suffix array, seeking candidates "that match the longest suffix of s"; continuations are inserted into a Trie weighted by prefix frequency; "we choose a subtree that contains the top c nodes with the highest weights"; the subtree is flattened by breadth-first search into a pseudo-sequence and verified under a tree attention mask so "any shared prefix appears only once". Acceptance is strict: "All correct tokens from the start will be accepted, and the draft tokens after the first mistake will be rejected" ([arXiv 2311.08252v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08252v2)).
- **Key tradeoff**: Quality of the draft is bounded by the datastore, not by a trained model — "the effectiveness of REST is affected by the quality of retrieval results" — and the match depth is capped: `n_max = 16`, justified only empirically ("only few cases lead to a maximum match with more than 16 tokens"). Retrieval cost is small but nonzero: "<6%" overhead. It is also explicitly confined to single-batch: "When benchmarked on 7B and 13B language models in a single-batch setting" ([arXiv 2311.08252v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08252v2), [arXiv 2311.08252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08252)).
- **What it got right that we have not**: REST publishes **absolute ms/token on named hardware next to the ratio**, so the wall-clock claim is auditable rather than asserted: "All the experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU and 96 CPU cores with a batch size of 1" ([REST README](https://github.com/FasterDecoding/REST)). It also shows that the drafting mechanism can be **swapped without retraining** — the datastore is rebuilt per domain (Python code from The Stack, ~2.7M samples; UltraChat, ~774K conversations) while the target model is untouched ([arXiv 2311.08252v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08252v2)). **(inference)** For a custom engine this is the cheapest available experiment for isolating "is the MTP head the bottleneck, or is the verify window's shape the bottleneck?" — a datastore drafter has no trained head to blame.
- **Metric it publishes**: `Mean Token Time (ms/token)` and `Speedup (×)`, per the results-table headers. Values, all single-batch: CodeLlama‑7B HumanEval (1-shot, greedy) 27.89 ms/token autoregressive → 11.82 ms/token REST = **2.36×** (speculative 15.90 ms/token = 1.75×); CodeLlama‑13B HumanEval (1-shot) 44.32 → 19.53 ms/token = 2.27×; CodeLlama‑7B HumanEval (10-shot, nucleus) 27.99 → 13.18 ms/token = **2.12×**; Vicuna‑7B MT-Bench (greedy) 25.48 → 15.12 ms/token = **1.69×**; Vicuna‑13B MT-Bench (greedy) 44.30 → 25.08 ms/token = **1.77×**; Vicuna‑7B MT-Bench (nucleus) 25.93 → 16.02 ms/token = **1.62×** ([ar5iv REST](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.08252)). Abstract headline: "REST achieves a significant speedup of 1.62× to 2.36× on code or text generation" ([arXiv 2311.08252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08252)). The paper's "Metrics" subsection text is **NOT VERIFIED** (see the note in §1).
- **Sources**: [ar5iv REST full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.08252) · [arXiv 2311.08252v2 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08252v2) · [arXiv 2311.08252v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08252v1) · [arXiv 2311.08252 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08252) · [github.com/FasterDecoding/REST](https://github.com/FasterDecoding/REST)

## 3. How the field defines the objective

### 3.1 Which metric each of the five uses as its headline

| system | acceptance rate | acceptance length | tokens per step | tokens per second | what it actually headlines |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Medusa | mentioned once, as the thing typical acceptance "boost[s]", but never tabulated | uses the phrase "expected acceptance length" to *design* the tree, never reports a τ column | — | yes, but only as the unit of the speedup it reports | **Speedup (×), measured wall-clock**, "2.3-3.6×", "2.18×/2.83×" tokens processed per second |
| EAGLE‑1/2/3 | reported in EAGLE‑1 as `0-α…4-α` (0.67–0.79); **explicitly dropped by EAGLE‑2 and absent from every results table in EAGLE‑2 and EAGLE‑3** | yes — the headline alongside speedup: τ = 6.62 (EAGLE‑3, Vicuna‑13B, T=0 mean) | this *is* τ, defined as "tokens accepted per forward pass" / "tokens generated per drafting-verification cycle" | yes in the framework sections (1.38× throughput at batch 64 in SGLang; 160.4 tokens/s on RTX 3090) | **Walltime speedup + average acceptance length τ** |
| Hydra | not reported as a headline; the ε sweep's goal is stated as quality preservation, not rate | yes, "average acceptance length… the number of tokens generated per decoding step" | same quantity as acceptance length | yes, "average throughput, which is the number of tokens generated per second" | **Average throughput (tokens/s) + average acceptance length** |
| Lookahead | treated as a theoretical *bound* on speedup, `E(β) = α`, with Appendix C deriving "Expectation of The Number of Accepted Tokens" from it | not tabulated as τ; the derived quantity is expected accepted tokens | yes, the central quantity: "generating N tokens in less than N steps", plus the W/N/G window | yes — all headlines are latency multipliers | **Wall-clock speedup + decoding steps per token** |
| REST | not used as a headline; acceptance is described only as the rule ("all correct tokens from the start") | not tabulated | implicit via matched continuation length, capped at n_max = 16 | yes — ms/token is a column in every results table | **Mean Token Time (ms/token) + Speedup (×)** |

### 3.2 The direct answer

**All five headline wall-clock speed or its hardware-independent proxy, acceptance length (equivalently, tokens
per step). None of the five headlines acceptance rate.** The four metric names are not four competing objectives;
they form a hierarchy, and each source says so in its own words:

1. **Tokens per second is the objective.** Hydra defines it as *throughput* and pairs it with acceptance length
   ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)); EAGLE defines it as the *walltime speedup ratio*
   and opens its metrics list by saying "EAGLE primarily focuses on latency rather than throughput"
   ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077)); REST reports ms/token
   ([ar5iv REST](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.08252)).
2. **Acceptance length / tokens-per-step is the portable proxy**, chosen precisely because wall clock is not
   portable: "The advantage of average acceptance length is that it is independent of hardware and runtime
   environment, while its disadvantage is that it does not reflect the overhead of the draft model"
   ([arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)). Hydra's is the most explicit definition found:
   "the average *acceptance length*, which is the number of tokens generated per decoding step"
   ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)). EAGLE‑2's is "**the average number of tokens
   generated per drafting-verification cycle, which corresponds to the number of tokens accepted from the
   draft**" ([arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)). vLLM, in production, names the same
   quantity `mean_acceptance_length` and defines it exactly: "Mean tokens emitted per verification step,
   including the bonus token: `1 + num_accepted_draft_tokens / num_spec_steps`. Ranges from `1.0` (nothing
   accepted) to `num_spec_tokens + 1`" ([vLLM acceptance metrics](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/speculative_decoding/acceptance_metrics/)).
   **(inference)** Strata's "tokens banked per verify window" is this metric; 2.2 → 2.5 is an acceptance-length
   *increase*, and the field would call that a win on the metric it uses to compare designs across hardware.
3. **Acceptance rate is a draft-model diagnostic.** Lookahead states the role it plays when it makes it a bound
   rather than a target: "their speedups are bounded by the *token acceptance rate* (§4.1), i.e., the fraction of
   tokens generated by the draft model that passes the verification test of the base model… In the worst case, if
   most proposed tokens fail verification, these methods may slow down the decoding process. Therefore, achieving
   a high acceptance rate is essential for these methods" ([arXiv 2402.02057v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02057v1)).
   Note the direction of the claim: acceptance rate bounds speedup *from above*; it is not itself a score.

### 3.3 Does any of them warn against using the acceptance RATE as a regression metric?

**Yes — explicitly, twice, and structurally a third time.**

- **EAGLE‑2 declines to publish it, with a stated reason.** Under a heading that is literally a question:
  "**Why is acceptance rate not included? The acceptance rate only reflects the performance of the draft model.
  Since EAGLE‑2 does not modify the structure of the draft model, the acceptance rate remains the same as that
  of EAGLE.**" ([arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)). This is the closest thing to a
  direct answer to the Strata question: a design change that raises acceptance length while leaving the ratio
  untouched is still counted as a pure win, and the ratio is simply not the reported quantity.
- **EAGLE‑1 calls it inapplicable to tree drafts.** In defining it, EAGLE writes: "**Acceptance rate α: The ratio
  of accepted to generated tokens during drafting, gauges draft accuracy. It's less applicable for tree drafts
  due to multiple tokens sampled per location with only one accepted.** Hence, when measuring this metric, we
  utilize chain drafts without tree attention" ([ar5iv EAGLE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.15077)).
  **(inference)** Strata's acceptance rate is "the accepted/tested draft *ratio*" over a verify window; when the
  floor drops, more of the tree survives, so the tested count rises — which is exactly the "multiple tokens
  sampled per location" situation EAGLE says the ratio cannot represent.
- **EAGLE‑3 and EAGLE‑2 rank eight methods without an acceptance-rate column.** Table 1 in both papers is
  "Speedup ratios and average acceptance lengths τ of different methods" and covers SpS, PLD, Medusa, Lookahead,
  Hydra, EAGLE, EAGLE‑2 (and EAGLE‑3), each with a speedup and a τ and nothing else
  ([arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3), [arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)).
  **(inference)** A field that used acceptance rate as a regression gate would not be able to rank its own
  methods' published results at all, because the number is not in the tables.

### 3.4 What this implies for the 0.7 → 0.5 decision

- **(inference) The measured pair is self-consistent and favourable under the field's objective.** Strata
  observed +8–11% throughput and tokens/window 2.2 → 2.5. Under vLLM's definition, tokens/window *is*
  `mean_acceptance_length`, whose documented range is 1.0 to k+1 and whose value rises when more draft tokens
  are accepted per step ([vLLM](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/speculative_decoding/acceptance_metrics/)).
  Both of the field's two headline axes moved up; only the diagnostic ratio moved down.
- **(inference) The falling ratio is arithmetic, not a quality signal.** Acceptance rate is
  `num_accepted_draft_tokens / num_draft_tokens` ([vLLM](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/speculative_decoding/acceptance_metrics/)) —
  a ratio with a moving denominator. Lowering the floor keeps more branches alive, so the number of *tested*
  draft tokens rises; if tested tokens rise faster than accepted tokens, the ratio falls while the absolute
  number banked per window grows. 91% → 87% with 2.2 → 2.5 tokens banked is that pattern exactly.
- **(inference) A pure chain model would predict the opposite sign, which is why the rate cannot be the chain α.**
  For a depth-4 chain with per-token acceptance probability α, expected accepted tokens is the geometric sum
  α + α² + α³ + α⁴, the quantity Lookahead derives in its appendix "Derivation of Expectation of The Number of
  Accepted Tokens" ([arXiv 2402.02057v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02057v1)). At α = 0.91 that gives ≈ 3.18; at
  α = 0.87, ≈ 2.86 — a *fall*. Strata measures a *rise*, so the logged acceptance rate is not the chain-α of that
  model and cannot be validated against it.
- **(inference) The published analogue of Strata's experiment scored the floor change on two axes, and neither
  was the ratio.** Hydra swept ε ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25} and plotted "both the average speculation length
  and the quality of the resulting generations" ([arXiv 2402.05109v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05109v2)).
  Adopting that protocol for the 0.7 → 0.5 change means: tokens/window and judged quality, printed side by side.
- **(inference) Two specific unclaimed wins sit in the sources.** First, Strata's floor is a bare constant, while
  Medusa's criterion is `min(ε, δ·exp(−H(p)))` — a fixed threshold *and* an entropy-scaled term, so acceptance
  widens automatically on flat distributions and the fixed value matters less
  ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)). At ε = 0.7 or 0.5 Strata is far above any published
  ε (Medusa and Hydra sweep 0.05–0.25), so the 5.5–11% found at 0.5 suggests there is remaining headroom that the
  entropy term would reach without lowering the constant at all. Second, Strata logs one blended acceptance rate;
  EAGLE‑3 logs it per position (`0-α…4-α`) and used exactly that resolution to find its own bottleneck, where a
  rising first-position rate masked a collapsing second-position rate
  ([arXiv 2503.01840v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)). Per-position reporting would show *which* of the
  four chain positions the 0.5 floor rescued and which it cost.
- **One caveat that cuts the other way (inference).** Typical acceptance is not lossless — Medusa says so in its
  own words ("we ascertain that it is typically unnecessary to match the distribution of the original model"),
  and EAGLE‑2 and EAGLE‑3 exclude Medusa from their temperature=1 tables for precisely that reason
  ([arXiv 2401.10774v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3), [arXiv 2406.16858v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858v2)).
  The floor is a quality knob, not only a throughput knob. That is the axis Hydra actually measured — judged
  generation quality alongside acceptance length — and it is the one axis Strata's six-metric rule does not
  currently cover for this change. **(inference)** If the floor moves, the standing law's M4 (MMLU-Pro) and M5
  (mimic, compliance, zero empties) are the metrics that can catch a distribution shift, and they should be run in
  the same window as the throughput measurement rather than assumed stable.
